FCA finalises PERG 18 crypto perimeter guidance as transitional application window nears

REGULATORY · CRYPTO · UK · Financial Conduct Authority Primary source

📜
What happened
On 16 September 2026 the FCA published PS26/18, its final guidance on when cryptoasset activities need FCA authorisation, which sits in the Handbook as PERG 18. The FCA states that from 25 October 2027 the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 (Cryptoassets) Regulations 2026 will introduce new regulated activities for cryptoassets into its perimeter, and that anyone wishing to carry on those activities by way of business in the UK will need to apply for authorisation unless a relevant exemption applies or they can use the savings or transitional run off provisions. The FCA also states that existing registrations and permissions will not convert automatically, and that the application window for firms wanting to use the transitional arrangements opens on 30 September 2026 and closes on 28 February 2027. PERG 18 describes itself as the FCA's view of how the statutory perimeter applies to regulated cryptoasset activities. It says it does not create, widen or narrow the regulated activities, specified investments, exclusions or exemptions set out in legislation, and that it does not bind the courts. The FCA adds that it plans to consult on further changes to the guidance in late 2026, following a Government statutory instrument amending the underlying regulations.
⚠
Why it matters
The perimeter is set by legislation. PERG 18 is the FCA's interpretation of it, and the FCA itself says the guidance cannot address every business model and that outcomes depend on the particular facts. That distinction matters for anyone reading the guidance as a checklist: it tells a firm how the regulator will approach the question, not what a court would necessarily decide. The guidance also says the new territorial rules can bring certain regulated cryptoasset activities involving UK consumers into the UK perimeter even where the provider is established overseas, so a provider's location is not a safe proxy for whether it needs UK authorisation. The transitional window is short, and the FCA's statement that existing registrations do not convert removes the assumption that a firm already registered under the Money Laundering Regulations is covered.
📡
Radar implication
This is a control judgement, not a finding by the FCA. Firms with UK crypto counterparties, VASP customers or crypto-linked payment flows should list which of those counterparties will need authorisation or a transitional route, and record whether each has said it intends to apply before the window closes on 28 February 2027. Do not treat an existing MLR registration as evidence that a counterparty will be authorised. For overseas counterparties that serve UK consumers, ask for their own perimeter analysis rather than relying on where they are incorporated, and escalate the ones that cannot give one. Keep the status of each analysis (counterparty view, your view, legal advice taken) on file, since PERG 18 is guidance and the FCA says perimeter outcomes are fact specific.

OFAC designates BitBank, its developer and three Zanjani associates in Iran digital asset action

ENFORCEMENT · SANCTIONS · US · US Department of the Treasury Primary source

📜
What happened
On 17 September 2026 the US Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) designated BitBank, which Treasury describes as an Iranian digital assets exchange controlled by OFAC-designated financier Babak Zanjani, together with its developer, Pishtaz Simorgh Electronic Trade Company, and three individuals Treasury calls associates of Zanjani: Hossein Ali Zaker Hossein, Mohammad Mahdi Zaker Hossein and Seyed Adel Heidari. Treasury states that all designations were taken pursuant to Executive Order 13902. It gives different bases within that order: BitBank and Pishtaz Simorgh for operating in the digital asset sector of the Iranian economy, and each of the three individuals for having acted or purported to act, directly or indirectly, for or on behalf of Zanjani, Pishtaz Simorgh and Dot One Value Creation Group respectively. Treasury describes the designated persons as key components of the Iranian regime's digital assets-based sanctions evasion infrastructure and states that OFAC-designated Hormuz Safe Marine Services Authority has used BitBank since June 2026 to transfer payments it received to the Iranian regime. Treasury points readers to OFAC FAQs 1250 and 1257 on sanctions risk associated with Iranian digital asset exchanges.
⚠
Why it matters
The action reaches both an exchange and the company Treasury identifies as its software developer, showing OFAC targeting operational components of the digital asset network rather than only named financiers or wallet addresses. Treasury's release says designated persons' property in the United States or in the possession or control of US persons is blocked and reportable to OFAC, and that entities owned 50 percent or more by blocked persons are also blocked. It also states that financial institutions and other persons may risk exposure to sanctions for engaging in certain transactions involving blocked persons, and that non-US persons are prohibited from causing US persons to violate US sanctions. Those are Treasury's statements of US law and enforcement posture. For non US firms, the applicable consequences are fact specific and should be assessed against the relevant OFAC authority, transaction and parties rather than inferred from place of incorporation alone.
📡
Radar implication
This is a control judgement, not a Treasury finding. Add the designated names and known BitBank and Pishtaz Simorgh identifiers to screening lists if your provider has not already done so, and search historic crypto payment and on-chain exposure data for direct or indirect links to BitBank. Read FAQs 1250 and 1257 before adjusting your Iranian digital asset exchange risk position. Non US firms should distinguish legal obligations from internal risk appetite and document how OFAC designations affect their sanctions, correspondent banking and USD clearing policies.

Alex Saab pleads guilty to conspiracy to launder proceeds of Venezuelan CLAP contract bribery scheme

ENFORCEMENT · CORRUPTION · US · US Department of Justice Primary source

📜
What happened
On 15 September 2026 the US Department of Justice announced that Alex Nain Saab Moran, a former Minister of Industry in the Maduro government, pleaded guilty to conspiracy to launder monetary instruments. The plea was heard by US District Judge Kathleen M. Williams in Miami. According to court documents, DOJ states that Saab carried out a nearly decade-long conspiracy to corruptly profit from Venezuela's CLAP food and medicine programme. As part of the plea, DOJ states that Saab admitted organising a system of bribes and other illegal payments to public officials so that entities secretly controlled by the conspirators could win Venezuelan government contracts, and that he used shell companies outside Venezuela to launder the proceeds around the world, including to the United States. DOJ also states that the conspirators used fake companies, false invoices, false shipping records and other fraudulent documents to skim hundreds of millions of dollars, but the release does not present that description as part of the plea. Saab faces a maximum penalty of 20 years in prison. DOJ states that a sentencing date has not been set and that a federal judge will determine any sentence after considering the US Sentencing Guidelines and other statutory factors.
⚠
Why it matters
The plea is to a laundering conspiracy, and DOJ's account shows how the proceeds are said to have been moved: shell companies outside Venezuela, false invoices and false shipping records that made trade in food and medicine look legitimate. Those are documentary controls a trade or correspondent bank would ordinarily rely on. The 20 year figure is the statutory maximum, not a sentence, and DOJ says a court will decide the sentence later. The DOJ description of the wider scheme is drawn from court documents and the release rather than from the plea itself, so it should be recorded with that limit.
📡
Radar implication
This is a control judgement, not a DOJ finding. For trade finance and correspondent relationships involving public-procurement supply of food, medicine or similar staples, test whether your file relies on invoices and shipping records alone, and whether you have independent evidence that the goods moved and that the beneficial owners of the counterparties are known. Check whether shell company counterparties in third countries are the ones paying or receiving on behalf of sanctioned or politically exposed principals. In adverse media records, log this as a guilty plea awaiting sentencing, not as a sentence, and keep the admitted matters separate from the allegations in court documents.

Five jailed for almost 40 years in total over Vietnamese people smuggling network that laundered over £1.6m

ENFORCEMENT · AML CTF · UK · National Crime Agency Primary source

📜
What happened
On 15 September 2026 five members of an organised crime group were sentenced at Liverpool Crown Court to a combined total of almost 40 years in an NCA case involving the facilitation of illegal entry by Vietnamese migrants and the laundering of more than £1.6m in cash from the criminal activity. The NCA worked jointly with French and Belgian law enforcement partners. The NCA states the group took migrants' passports in France, used them as a form of debt bondage, collected money from the migrants and sent payments back to Vietnam through Post Offices and money transfer bureaux including Western Union, MoneyGram and Ria. Production Orders served on those three providers showed more than £1.65m transferred across 740 transactions to accounts in Vietnam between July 2024 and October 2025. The NCA also states that migrants were placed in workplaces under debt bondage arrangements, including nail salons referred to in the group's messages, and that many worked in the grey economy after arriving. Individual sentences ranged from four years to 12 years and six months.
⚠
Why it matters
The laundering here was low-tech and high-volume: 740 transfers through regulated money service channels over roughly 15 months, funded by cash the NCA says was collected from various locations across the UK. Nothing in the NCA account suggests a complex structure, which is the point: the exposure sat in the pattern across many small transactions and senders, not in any single payment. The case also shows organised immigration crime generating a laundering channel as well as people smuggling activity, with debt repayments as the source of the cash.
📡
Radar implication
This is a control judgement, not an NCA finding, and it does not make migrant-related cash or remittances suspicious in themselves. For money service business and bank monitoring, the indicators worth testing are repeated cash deposits or remittances to the same destination country by multiple unrelated senders, sender clusters using the same accounts or receipt patterns across several Post Office or agent locations, and cash collections from small businesses such as nail bars that are passed on to individuals rather than banked through the business. Where a business account is linked to a cash-collecting pattern, look at the wider profile before filing: who owns and staffs the business, whether staff pay looks consistent with the sector, and whether any debt bondage or forced labour indicators are present. Document why a pattern is or is not suspicious rather than relying on nationality or destination country.

OFAC designates 36 targets across Iran's civil aviation network under 'Operation Economic Outcast'

ENFORCEMENT · SANCTIONS · US · US Department of the Treasury Primary source

📜
What happened
On 8 September 2026, the US Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) designated 36 targets under an action Treasury branded 'Operation Economic Outcast'. Twenty-seven are Iranian airlines, including Iran Air Tour, Iran Aseman Airlines, Kish Airlines, Qeshm Air and Saha Airlines, designated under Executive Order 13902 for operating in Iran's aviation sector. Of the nine remaining targets, eight entities and one individual, Ibrahim Ali Mohamed Mohamed Mahran, based across the UAE, Türkiye, Malaysia, Kazakhstan and the UK, were designated under Executive Order 13224, as amended, for roles including supporting Mahan Air, aircraft management, cargo services, logistics and general sales agent activity. Treasury cited different bases for different targets within that group, including material support, ownership or control, and leadership or official status, rather than a single uniform basis. Treasury separately suspended three general licences previously available under the Iranian Transactions and Sanctions Regulations. All property and interests in property of designated persons within US jurisdiction are blocked, and US persons are generally prohibited from transacting with them.
⚠
Why it matters
This is a coordinated network designation, not a single-entity listing: OFAC paired direct designations of the Iranian airlines themselves with designations of third-country aviation-support businesses that keep those airlines operating, spread across five separate jurisdictions. For institutions with aviation, leasing, insurance or logistics exposure in the Gulf, Turkey or Central Asia, screening only against the airline names themselves misses the support layer this action specifically targets.
📡
Radar implication
Re-screen aviation-adjacent counterparties, freight forwarders, aircraft lessors and cargo logistics providers in the UAE, Türkiye, Malaysia and Kazakhstan against the newly designated entities, not only against the Iranian airline names. One quarter of the targets, 9 of 36, were based outside Iran, illustrating how aviation-related sanctions exposure extends into third-country corporate and intermediary networks that a jurisdiction-based Iran filter alone would not catch. If your firm relied on any of the three now-suspended Iranian Transactions and Sanctions Regulations general licences, confirm wind-down authority and any applicable deadline directly against OFAC's own suspension notice before processing further transactions.

FinCEN links $17.5bn in suspicious activity to health care fraud, flags Puerto Rico Medicaid balance-manipulation pattern

CRITICAL · FRAUD · US · FinCEN Primary source

📜
What happened
FinCEN published a Financial Trend Analysis, issued pursuant to Section 6206 of the Anti-Money Laundering Act of 2020, analysing 5,702 Bank Secrecy Act reports filed by 471 financial institutions between 1 March 2025 and 28 February 2026 that identified approximately $17.5bn in suspicious activity linked to suspected health care fraud (completed and attempted transactions combined). Depository institutions filed 89% of the reports and accounted for 87% of the dollar volume. Home health care businesses were the most frequently identified type of suspected fraudulent provider, appearing in over a fifth of the dataset. FinCEN separately detailed a Puerto Rico-specific pattern in which Medicaid recipients allegedly withdrew funds shortly before an eligibility review to obtain a bank balance certification letter showing they qualified, then redeposited the funds within days; 59 Puerto Rican financial cooperatives filed 1,641 of the reports in the dataset, almost entirely describing this pattern.
⚠
Why it matters
This is FinCEN's own aggregation of activity institutions are already filing, not a new typology firms are being asked to start looking for: the $17.5bn figure tells firms the scale of health care fraud-linked activity already sitting in SAR data industry-wide. The Puerto Rico balance-certification pattern is a mechanically specific scheme, funds pulled down before a review and restored days later, that a standard high-balance or high-turnover alert would likely miss, since neither the withdrawal nor the redeposit looks unusual in isolation.
📡
Radar implication
If your transaction monitoring rules key off home health care, hospice, durable medical equipment or behavioural health provider codes, check whether a rule already exists for a short-window withdrawal-then-redeposit pattern timed against a customer-stated benefits review, rather than only generic high-cash-turnover thresholds. For institutions with Puerto Rico-based accounts, consider a dedicated look-back at cooperativa-filed SARs matching this pattern, since FinCEN's own data shows it concentrated overwhelmingly in that filer population. Confirm your SAR narrative templates for health care fraud already distinguish billing fraud (upcoding, phantom billing) from proceeds-placement typologies like this one, since the appropriate control response differs.

NCA's National Economic Crime Centre ranks cryptoassets third among nine national economic-crime priorities in new annual report

REGULATORY · CRYPTO · UK · National Crime Agency (National Economic Crime Centre) Primary source

📜
What happened
The NCA's National Economic Crime Centre (NECC) published its 2025-26 Annual Report, confirming cryptoassets as the third-ranked priority among the nine 'Economic Crime System Priorities' agreed with the FCA, HM Treasury and the Home Office as part of Economic Crime Plan 2 (the priorities themselves were published in July 2025; only professional enablers and politically exposed persons rank above cryptoassets). The report states the NECC is 'developing a more proactive and intelligence-led crypto capability' and cites 'the innovative use of crypto asset products to evade detection and move illicit value at scale' as a growing feature of the illicit finance landscape. It also updates figures for Operation Destabilise, the NECC's investigation into Russian-speaking cash-to-crypto laundering networks: 129 arrests and over £25m seized in cash and cryptoassets in the UK since 2022, plus a further $24m and over €2.6m seized internationally using intelligence the operation generated.
⚠
Why it matters
This is a status confirmation of an existing priority ranking rather than a new designation, but the operational detail behind it, an intelligence-led crypto capability the NECC describes as still being built rather than already operating at scale, matters for how firms read NECC alerts and information requests in this space. The Destabilise figures also point to a shift in the laundering model: criminal cash is being converted to crypto specifically to move value across borders, not only to obscure it.
📡
Radar implication
If your firm's crypto exposure risk rating still treats crypto as a peripheral or emerging risk category, this report is the NECC's own basis for treating it as a top-three national priority alongside professional enablers and PEPs, worth reflecting explicitly in your next risk assessment refresh. For firms handling cash-intensive businesses with subsequent crypto conversion, review whether your typologies cover a distinct 'cash-to-crypto swap' pattern rather than only conventional layering, since that is the specific mechanism the NECC attributes to the Destabilise network.

NCA-wanted suspect arrested at Dublin Airport over alleged $23m fraud on Australian mining company

ENFORCEMENT · FRAUD · UK · National Crime Agency Primary source

📜
What happened
Edwin Omokhuale, 39, was arrested by An Garda Síochána at Dublin Airport on 9 September 2026 as he attempted to board a flight to Paris, acting on an NCA warrant. The NCA states he is wanted in connection with money laundering linked to an alleged fraud of approximately $23m (around £17m) against an Australian mining company in early 2023, in which company employees were allegedly deceived into transferring funds to accounts controlled by UK-based fraudsters. Omokhuale, who lived in the UK at the time of the alleged fraud, appeared before a Dublin court on 10 September 2026 as extradition proceedings to the UK began. No charges have been determined by a UK court and the allegations remain unproven.
⚠
Why it matters
The fraud allegedly ran through UK-based conduits, but the victim, an Australian mining company, and the point of arrest, Dublin, are both outside the UK, illustrating how a single fraud allegation can span three jurisdictions before an extradition even resolves who tries the case. For firms handling payments connected to mining or resources-sector counterparties, this is a reminder that business email compromise-style schemes are not confined to smaller corporates.
📡
Radar implication
If your firm processes payments for mining, resources or extractives clients where instructions reference account changes or new counterparties, confirm your payment verification callback procedure requires an independently sourced phone number, not one supplied in the instruction itself, since that is the standard vector for this fraud pattern. Treat this as an open extradition matter in any adverse media screening: record it as wanted with unresolved allegations and extradition proceedings in progress, not as a conviction or a finalised fraud finding.

OFSI fines Citibank's London branch £4.73m over sustained Russia sanctions breaches

ENFORCEMENT · SANCTIONS · UK · HM Treasury (OFSI) Primary source

📜
What happened
OFSI imposed a £4,732,830.58 civil monetary penalty on Citibank, N.A.'s London branch under section 146 of the Policing and Crime Act 2017, for breaches of the Russia (Sanctions) (EU Exit) Regulations 2019 and the Global Anti-Corruption Sanctions Regulations 2021. Across eight separate breach categories, the bank processed 970 payments worth a combined £19.7m involving designated persons and banks including Alfa-Bank, Gazprombank and Credit Bank of Moscow, largely in the year following Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine. OFSI rated the case Level 4, its most serious category, and applied a 40% combined discount for voluntary disclosure, cooperation and early settlement against a baseline penalty of £7.89m.
⚠
Why it matters
This is one of the most detailed public OFSI penalty notices to date on how a major correspondent bank's sanctions controls actually failed under sustained pressure, not through deliberate evasion but through screening calibration gaps, alert backlogs and slow escalation. The notice gives firms a concrete benchmark for reviewing whether sanctions screening, alert handling and escalation controls can withstand a sudden increase in designation volume and operational pressure.
📡
Radar implication
Two specific control gaps are worth checking against your own systems: first, whether your screening logic correctly matches designated entities that appear in your KYC records under a variant legal form (OFSI found Citibank's system treated 'Sovcomflot' and 'PAO Sovcomflot' as different names, missing 32 accounts entirely). Second, whether your correspondent banking payment chain is re-screened after correspondent banks are added automatically, not only at initial payment creation, since Citibank's screening ran before its own payment processor inserted the correspondent bank into 19 payments to designated Russian institutions. Also review your frozen-asset reporting SLAs: OFSI treated a 274-day average delay across 53 reports as an aggravating factor in its own right.

FinCEN flags $12.7bn in suspected 'pig butchering' crypto scam activity since 2023

CRITICAL · CRYPTO · US · FinCEN Primary source

📜
What happened
FinCEN analysed 33,904 Bank Secrecy Act reports filed between September 2023 and December 2025 and identified approximately $12.7bn in financial activity linked to suspected 'pig butchering' and romance-baiting investment scams, run largely by transnational criminal groups operating scam compounds in Southeast Asia. The alert details the scam lifecycle, from fake investment platforms and 'guarantee marketplaces' selling laundering and phishing services, through shell companies, money mules and stablecoin transfers, and encourages 314(b) information sharing between institutions.
⚠
Why it matters
This is a scale confirmation, not a new typology: FinCEN is telling institutions that reported pig-butchering activity has already reached a size that should be showing up in aggregate suspicious-activity trends, not just isolated case referrals. Firms that haven't yet mapped this typology into their transaction monitoring rules are working from an outdated risk picture.
📡
Radar implication
Check whether your TM rules and SAR narratives already reference the specific red flags FinCEN lists, romance or relationship-initiated contact preceding a funding request, 'guarantee marketplace' style third-party facilitation fees, and rapid conversion through stablecoins to obscure the trail, rather than generic 'crypto exposure' flags. If your CDD refresh triggers don't currently include a pattern of small test transfers followed by escalating amounts to a single new counterparty, that's the specific behavioural sequence this alert describes and worth adding now, alongside considering whether Section 314(b) information sharing is appropriate with eligible participating financial institutions where the activity may involve money laundering or terrorist activity.

SEC charges Pacific Private Money Group executives in $80m+ Ponzi-like real estate fund scheme

ENFORCEMENT · FRAUD · US · US Securities and Exchange Commission Primary source

📜
What happened
The SEC charged Mark D. Hanf, former CEO of Pacific Private Money Group LLC, and Hoai-Nam Chu Phan (Nam Phan), former COO of a PPMG subsidiary, over a Ponzi-like scheme run from December 2021 to November 2025. The Novato, California-based fund raised over $80m from roughly 190 mostly retail investors, many retired senior citizens, on promises of guaranteed returns from real estate-secured loans. The SEC alleges returns were largely paid from new investor money rather than genuine loan income, that Hanf personally misappropriated more than $7m, and that outstanding investor obligations of approximately $121m are backed by less than $17m in recoverable assets.
⚠
Why it matters
The victim profile, retired, mostly retail investors relying on a 'guaranteed return' real estate product, is the same demographic regulators keep flagging as high-risk for unregulated or semi-regulated investment schemes. The SEC alleges the conduct continued from December 2021 until November 2025, illustrating how a private fund can continue attracting and servicing investors for years before an alleged funding mismatch becomes publicly visible.
📡
Radar implication
For firms exposed to private real estate lending funds, promised fixed returns combined with limited visibility into underlying loan performance and funding flows should trigger closer due diligence. The SEC's allegation here is that returns were funded largely from new investor capital rather than earnings from the stated lending activity.

FCA decides to ban and fine adviser £742,700 over unauthorised pension transfer advice

ENFORCEMENT · FRAUD · UK · Financial Conduct Authority Primary source

📜
What happened
The FCA has decided to ban Daniel Thomas, a director at DPT Financial Solutions Limited (an appointed representative of Quilter Financial Services), from working in financial services and fine him £742,700 after provisionally finding that he recklessly gave defined benefit pension transfer advice he was neither qualified nor permitted to provide. Thomas has referred the Decision Notice to the Upper Tribunal, so the findings remain provisional and the FCA will take no action until the Tribunal reaches its decision. Thomas advised 53 clients across 63 transfer cases, some of them British Steel Pension Scheme members the FCA regards as particularly vulnerable, earning over £173,000 in fees. The FCA provisionally found that he misled clients and pension providers about his qualifications, separately provided misleading information to his principal firm about his involvement in the pension transfer cases, destroyed client records, and failed to cooperate with the investigation.
⚠
Why it matters
This case combines two of the FCA's longest-running concerns, unqualified individuals giving DB transfer advice through an appointed representative structure, and evidence destruction to obstruct supervision, in a single enforcement action against a named individual rather than just the firm.
📡
Radar implication
The FCA provisionally found that Thomas misled clients and pension providers about his qualifications, and separately provided misleading information to his principal firm about his involvement in the pension transfer cases, two distinct misrepresentations to two different audiences. If your firm operates an appointed representative network for regulated advice, particularly DB pension transfers, check whether your oversight verifies each adviser's individual qualification status against the specific permission being exercised independently of what the AR itself reports, and whether your record-retention controls would detect an adviser selectively destroying client files.
